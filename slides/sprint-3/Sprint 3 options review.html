Four directions for natural-language search, and what each one costs.

Four clickable prototypes, each answering a different position on how much of the search experience the AI should take over. This deck is the argument around them: the pros, the cons, the click counts read out of the code, and the links to click into any state of any flow.

Every prototype is live at globaldata-design.vercel.app/sprint-3. Sample data throughout; what is wired and what is not is stated on each screen.

03

Who we are designing for

Three goals, and the failure mode is serving one of them.

Goal Who Volume What they need from the interface
G1 · Quick screen Most searches, most users ~70% of queries A cold start that reaches a usable list fast. "I've got in my head what I want to screen for and go for it." Minimum clicks, minimum reading, ideally one sentence.
G2 · Refine Power users with a list that is nearly right The ~30% refined Cheap, reversible, legible refinement without starting over. "That's not quite what I wanted, let me tailor that precisely."
G3 · Deep Boolean Advanced users at your largest accounts ~10% of refined Full Boolean across attribute groups — AND, OR, NOT, cross-attribute logic, nesting. The granularity they are paying for.

A direction that serves one goal and abandons the others fails, and so does one that serves them as two products bolted together.

04

The standing question

The fusion — and the honest answer that none of the four has it yet.

I'd actually thought we were going to end up closer to an iteration of this and maybe sidebar together… some clever fusion of classic Boolean search building and ability for natural language searches, so that the searches that can be triggered just with a natural language thing get going, and the rest that needed detail refinement, we blended the interface. Neil Dodgson, 14 September

Each of the four is a position on that spectrum rather than an attempt at the middle of it, and that was deliberate — the point of the week was range. What follows names, for each one, exactly where the fusion breaks and what it would take to close it. Those four "what would close it" sections are the real proposal in this deck.

The gap that is common to all four

None of the four can express a genuinely nested Boolean query — a group of ANDs OR'd against another group. Your live platform can. G3 is the segment paying for that, so we are flagging it rather than letting you find it: whichever direction wins, cross-attribute OR and grouping have to be built into the query model underneath it.

06

Option 1 · the baseline

Modal / Sidebar Takeover

You land on the full drug table and filtering is a separate mode you enter. One overlay, two tabs over one shared builder: ask in English on the AI tab, or click down three levels of menu on the Manual tab. Either way you press Apply, and the query reappears as pills above the table. This is the design reviewed on 2 September, rebuilt so it runs — here as the baseline the other three are measured against, not as a candidate.

Pros

  • Natural language and hand-building write into one object. A pill the AI wrote reopens the same picker path a hand-built one does. Precedent: HubSpot's list builder puts "Use AI to create filters" above nested Boolean groups. It is a partial answer to the fusion question, and it is under-sold in the current build. G1→G3
  • Counts sit next to every value before you spend a click. Your live platform shows no number anywhere before you commit. Baymard call per-value counts the single highest-impact filter improvement. G1 G2
  • The applied query is legible and editable from the bar — a pill re-filters on tick with no Apply. G2
  • Boolean as visible controls, not typed syntax. Most users cannot write Boolean; beehiiv, Proton and HubSpot all ship the control form instead.

Cons

  • The cold start is 3n + 3 clicks — it matches the live platform rather than beating it. One field costs 6, four fields cost 15. It fails G1 on your first measure.
  • The modal hides the table and shows no total until Apply, and the per-value counts are corpus-wide constants rather than conditioned on what is already picked.
  • Three stacked cascade levels, one branch at a time — your own objection, and the research agrees: collapsing six menu levels to two cut errors from 12.5% to 2.2%.
  • The Boolean ceiling is lower than the controls suggest. Verified in the code: between-group OR is drawn and then ignored by the filter, and NOT prefixes the following group rather than joining. No parentheses. Fails G3.
  • A second question replaces the query rather than amending it, so the AI tab can start a query but not refine one. Fails G2, and it is why the fusion is half-built.
  • Past ten values the bar folds into +N whose only action is reopening the modal — the G3 query is exactly the one that stops being visible.
Verdict. The direction for G2 — someone nudging one value on a list they already have. Its single structural problem is that filtering is a mode that hides the results it filters, so G1's fast start and G3's visible depth are shut behind the same door.

07

Option 2 · the one you were drawn to

Miller Columns

The filter taxonomy drills sideways instead of down. Each level opens as a new column beside the last, nothing is ever wiped, every row carries the count it would leave you with, and the results sit beside the columns rather than behind them. An agent in the panel drives those same columns in place, so you watch it do what a person would.

Pros

  • The path stays on screen and nothing is wiped — precisely the "I lose the context, I can't have multiple things open" complaint, and precisely what the live screener does wrong. G2 G3
  • Counts on every attribute and every value, recomputed with that attribute's own condition lifted. Six clicks against the live platform's 13–16 plus a blind six-second wait. G1 G2
  • No commit step. Count, pills and table all derive from one filtered set, so they cannot disagree and refinement costs one click. G2
  • The filter surface is independent of the grid — it opens filtered on an attribute no column shows. That is the argument against Option 4. G2 G3
  • The agent drives the real controls, step by step, each step individually undoable, and it yields the moment you touch a column. Precedent: Notion AI Autofill, Databricks Assistant, Clay's Sculptor.

Cons

  • There is no cold start. The composer sits at the foot of the panel, below the taxonomy — for a three-field query the slow instrument is in front of you and the fast one is below the fold. This is your objection, and it is correct.
  • Nothing sequences the task. Our own reservation, put plainly: one value row has three outcomes depending on which pixel you hit. Learnable in thirty seconds, invisible in the first five.
  • It is running at about half the real density — 15 attributes against roughly 27, 13 therapy areas against 24 — so the horizontal-versus-vertical bet is untested at full size.
  • "Three columns" only renders above 1880px. At the 1440px review viewport you get two.
  • Attributes are hard-ANDed. (a OR b) AND (c OR d) AND NOT e works; cross-attribute OR, grouping and nesting do not. G3 is not served.
  • Almost no shipped web precedent for cascading columns, and the keyboard traversal that makes the pattern fast on a desktop is not built.
Verdict. The strongest structure of the four and the weakest opening. Keep it, and move the composer from the foot of the panel to the front of the journey — so the first query is a sentence and the columns are what the sentence lands in.

08

Option 2 · what would close the gap

The cold start, fixed without losing what you liked.

Four moves. None of them touches the column structure you responded to.

1 · The ask becomes the empty state

Put the composer at the top of the panel with the columns quiet beneath it, collapsing to a persistent bar once a query exists. Shipped this way in Indeed Smart Sourcing and Todoist's filter assistant.

2 · The sentence lands in the columns

The agent already does this. So the first query and the lesson in how the taxonomy works become the same event — which is the one thing Option 3 cannot do.

3 · An explicit hand-off line

At the end of a run, offer widen and narrow. That answers "how does it flip into the power-user view" for free, because the power view is already on screen.

4 · Follow-ups against the standing query

"Now only Phase III", "drop Italy". Type, click, type becomes one instrument rather than two modes.

Stated honestly: none of this fixes G3. Cross-attribute OR and grouping have to be built into the query model whichever direction wins.

Option 3 · the AI-native one

Text Input Field

Natural language takes the whole stage. You type a request in your own words and it comes back as the query itself, written as one editable line of English — every value a pill with a dropdown, every Boolean operator a word you can change. A Filters toggle swaps the same clauses into a conventional view in place, rather than adding a second surface.

Pros

  • The cold start is genuinely zero clicks. The composer is focused on arrival and Enter submits. Three clauses and 23,200 drugs from one sentence. G1
  • The prompt resolves into controls you can see, which is the documented remedy for people not knowing what they are allowed to ask; explaining the parse back measurably improves user correctness. G1 G2
  • Edits are priced right — two or three clicks to correct a value, one to drop a clause, one undo step per edit. G2
  • The only direction that answers your question with one object. The Filters view imports its controls from the sentence, so the swap is provably lossless — it is the same query drawn twice, not two queries to reconcile.
  • It uses the good search you already built and orphaned. The header's natural-language search hands off into a screener in one click.

Cons

  • It misreads queries in ways that read as correct. Verified by running the real resolver — see the next slide. This is the most serious finding in the deck.
  • Users will not check. Measured trust in natural-language query tools runs 6.1 out of 7 against 4.0 for the native interface, with self-described light verification. Fluent output plus light checking is how a wrong number reaches a client deck.
  • A blank input teaches nothing. Four suggestions and ten attribute names against roughly 27 real attributes. Option 2 shows you all of them; this shows you none.
  • Nested Boolean is not expressible and is silently flattened. Mitigating: your live platform cannot do it either. Fails G3.
  • Adding a condition applies an unrequested default and moves the count; a non-default extra condition costs five clicks, worse than the live platform.
  • No way to refine from the results. The grid sorts and nothing more.
Verdict. The fastest cold start of the four and the only single-object answer to the natural-language question — but it tops out at your current flat Boolean ceiling and hides its misreads inside fluent English. The right front door for the 70%, not yet a home for the 10%.

Option 3 · the trust problem, measured

Three real misreads, from the real resolver.

We compiled the prototype's parser and ran queries through it. These are its outputs, not our speculation — and each one is confidently, fluently wrong.

What was typedWhat came backWhat went wrong
marketed in Germany but not approved in Japan “only in Germany or Japan, not in the Marketed stage” Mixed polarity collapsed — one clause per attribute by construction
…marketed in the US, excluding Japan “excluding the United States or Japan” The exclusion swallowed the inclusion
non-generic drugs “Generic drugs” The negation was dropped entirely

These are fixable — they are a consequence of one clause per attribute, not of natural language as an idea. But they are the reason this direction needs per-clause provenance before it can be trusted with a screening decision: show which words produced which condition, the way PubMed's Search Details does, so trust becomes inspection rather than faith.

Option 3 · what would close the gap

The flip into the power view — and why the Filters tab is not it.

You asked to see how the natural-language view hands off to the power user. The honest finding is that today's Filters tab does not do it: it redraws the same query at the same expressive power. What would is a three-rung ladder on one query object — tabs, not a modal, with the results table never moving.

Rung 1 · Sentence G1

Unchanged. Owns the 70% at zero clicks.

Rung 2 · Filters G2

Re-scoped to earn its tab: several clauses per attribute, which fixes the Germany/Japan misread, plus real operators — is none of, comparators. Flat AND.

Rung 3 · Builder G3

New. A nested group tree, each group all / any / none. The only rung that holds (Phase III AND oral) OR (Marketed AND injectable).

The mechanic that makes it a fusion

The composer stays live inside the builder, so a typed phrase adds a group at any depth. Climbing is free because a flat list is a degenerate group tree; descending renders a nested group as a read-only "matching either of 2 groups" pill and never silently drops logic.

The precedent to look at is Linear: its Add Filter menu lists AI Filter and Advanced filter as the top two entries above the attribute list, and both write into one chip row. Tableau's retirement of Ask Data in 2024 is the negative precedent worth acknowledging.

Option 4 · the results-first one

Sidebar Agent

No filter surface at all. You land in the grid and filter from the column headers, Excel-style, with counts in every menu — and a docked assistant acts on that same grid, proposing filters, columns, sorting and grouping. You accept, the steps land, and the thread keeps a receipt with an undo. It is also the only direction that redesigns the results view itself.

Pros

  • It is the only direction that answers your grid complaint — "the grid falls off the side of the screen" — and the fix is portable underneath any of the other three.
  • Two clicks per parameter with scope-correct counts before you commit, so the zero-hit query stops happening. G1 G2
  • The Excel idiom at the top level, which is what your users actually have in their hands all day.
  • Propose, preview the row count, land the steps, leave a receipt with an undo — the textbook shape for a mixed-initiative agent, and shipped that way in Descript, Asana and Lightfield. G2
  • Column management your live product does not have — nine of 37 columns shown, each hideable, movable and pinnable. No modes, no Apply, no round trip.

Cons

  • The cold start, which is your objection. The build opens with three filters already applied by the assistant, which quietly hides the real first screen — every row in the database and nine column headers to guess at. We have added that state as its own link (cold-grid) so you can judge it rather than take our word for it. Fails G1.
  • The BI-assistant pattern has the worst retention record of any here. Tableau retired Ask Data in 2024; Power BI's Q&A goes in February 2027. In one study a chatbot was rated more useful and 100% of participants still chose the dashboard when confidence mattered.
  • You can only filter what is on screen. Eleven of fourteen columns are filterable, drug name and brand are not filterable at all, and the sort and group menus list visible columns only.
  • The Boolean model is the thinnest of the four — OR within a column, AND across them, and no NOT anywhere. Fails G3 outright.
  • Undo is whole-grid, so it reverts hand edits made since.
  • The panel eats 380px of a grid whose stated problem is width.
Verdict. The grid redesign is the most valuable single artefact in Sprint 3 and should be adopted whichever filtering model wins. The results-first filtering model, as built, fails the cold start you named and cannot express the depth your highest-value customers pay for — so it continues only with a screener on its front and Boolean moved into the filter pills.

What we are asking you to decide

Three questions, and the links to answer them with.

1 · Which front door?

A sentence that resolves into structure (Option 3), or a browsable taxonomy with a sentence on its front (Option 2). Both reach a three-field list in under six clicks; they teach the user completely different things on the way.

2 · Does the grid redesign go in regardless?

Option 4's results view answers a complaint none of the others touch, and it is independent of which filtering model wins.

3 · How much Boolean do the 10% actually need?

None of the four expresses nested, cross-attribute logic today, and building it is real work. We need your read on what the largest accounts genuinely construct before we design for it.

What we would do next, unprompted

Fuse Option 2 and Option 3: a composer at the front that resolves a sentence into the columns, the columns as the power view it lands in, and a group builder underneath for the 10%. That is the blended interface you described expecting — and both halves already exist as running prototypes.
